Memory ◆ Entropy An explainer

The physics of memory

Memory is a local rebellion against entropy.

A record is a fragile pocket of order in a universe that is running down. Forming one, keeping one, even erasing one — each has a price, and the price is set by thermodynamics. Here is the physics of remembering, from the arrow of time to the demon who guards the second law.

The molecules spread and never regather. That one-way drift — not any law of the individual collisions — is the arrow of time. The meter reads the Shannon entropy of their arrangement; it rises, then holds.

I

Statistics, not law

Time points the way disorder does

The microscopic laws of physics run the same forwards and backwards; watch two molecules collide and you cannot tell which way the film is playing. Yet a drop of ink in water spreads and never gathers back into a drop. Why the asymmetry?

Because there are vastly more disordered arrangements of many particles than ordered ones. An isolated system, nudged at random, drifts — overwhelmingly probably — toward the more numerous states. The “direction of time” is just the direction of that drift. Entropy counts those arrangements.

S = kB ln W

S entropy of the macrostate  ·  W number of microscopic arrangements that look the same  ·  kB Boltzmann’s constant. The second law: for an isolated system, S does not fall.

The gas above is this sentence, made moving. Every collision is reversible; the spreading is not.

II

The starting line

Something had to be tidy first

Here is the twist that undoes a lazy answer. The statistics behind the second law are themselves time-symmetric: applied to a typical present, they predict higher entropy in both directions — future and past alike. On its own, the law gives you a valley, not an arrow.

To get a real past, you must add one stark assumption: the early universe began in an extraordinarily low-entropy state. Physicists call it the Past Hypothesis. It relocates the mystery — the deep fact is not that entropy rises, but that it started so improbably low, and everything since has been running downhill from that one tidy beginning.

Every memory you will ever form is a debt drawn against the low-entropy start of the universe.

III

Records only point back

Why we remember the past, never the future

A memory is a physical system whose state is reliably correlated with an earlier event — a photograph, a groove in a synapse, a flashcard. Laying down that correlation requires the recording medium to start in a special, blank, low-entropy “ready” state and end up marked by what it recorded. That process always raises total entropy.

So reliable records can be written only in the direction entropy increases — toward what we call the future. Records therefore accumulate about the past and never about the future. The past simply is the temporal direction we hold records of. The felt arrow of memory is a child of the thermodynamic arrow, which is a child of that tidy beginning.

This is why a spaced-repetition system can exist at all. Learning from the past is not a given. It is a privilege granted by physics — and, like every record, it comes with a bill.

IV

The price of forgetting

Erasing a memory costs heat

To erase a bit is to take many possible prior states and map them onto one — reset to 0. That collapse shrinks the number of arrangements the memory can be in, which lowers its entropy. And the second law will not permit a free lunch: the lost entropy must be paid out to the surroundings as heat.

E ≥ kBT ln 2

Landauer’s bound, per bit erased. T absolute temperature of the surroundings; ln 2 is the cost of collapsing two states into one. At room temperature that floor is about 2.9 × 10−21 J — tiny, strictly non-zero, and measured in the lab (Bérut et al., 2012).

A subtle point rescues computation from the same fate: it is erasure — discarding information — that costs, not calculation itself.

Where the cost actually lives

Computation

No fundamental floor.

Logically reversible steps keep every bit, so in principle they can run on arbitrarily little energy. Reversible computing lives here.

Erasure

At least kBT ln 2 per bit.

Many-to-one, logically irreversible, unavoidably dissipative. The price is for forgetting — Bennett’s correction to the old folklore that every logic gate must burn energy.

V

The demon’s debt

What actually saves the second law

Maxwell imagined a demon at a trapdoor between two chambers of gas, letting fast molecules through one way and slow ones the other — sorting hot from cold, lowering the gas’s entropy for free, apparently breaking the second law. Watch the ledger: every molecule the demon sorts is one observation it must store — and every stored bit is one it must eventually erase, at a cost of at least kBT ln 2. The order it creates and the debt it owes tick up together. The gas gets more ordered; the universe does not.

The resolution took a century. The demon must remember what it observes to decide how to sort, and its memory is finite. To keep working it must eventually erase old records — and by Landauer’s principle, each erased bit dumps at least kBT ln 2 of heat outward. That erasure cost meets or exceeds every scrap of entropy the demon skimmed off the gas. Total entropy still rises.

The law is saved not by the cost of measuring, but by the cost of forgetting.

VI

The synthesis

Learning is buying order with heat

Now the whole chain closes. A durable memory — the very thing a review schedule is built to protect — is a low-entropy, ordered correlation between a system and a past fact: more ordered than the same matter left to chance. The second law permits that local order only because a larger disorder is exported to pay for it: metabolic heat, the energy to write, to review, to re-consolidate.

Learning never breaks the second law. It reverses entropy locally at a net global cost — the same trade that keeps a cell alive or a refrigerator cold. And the two entropies in that sentence are, at bottom, one quantity.

Two entropies, one currency

Shannon (information)

H = −Σ pi log2 pi

Missing information about which state a system is in, measured in bits. pi is the probability of state i.

Thermodynamic (Gibbs)

S = −kB Σ pi ln pi

The same uncertainty over physical microstates, scaled by kB into joules per kelvin. Same quantity, up to kB ln 2 per bit.

That identity is the hinge. It is what lets Landauer convert “one erased bit” into “kBT ln 2 joules.” Information has a temperature. Spaced repetition, then, is a schedule for repaying a memory’s entropy debt — re-lowering the local disorder the universe is forever trying to raise, one review before the trace fades.
